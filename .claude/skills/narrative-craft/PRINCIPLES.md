# Craft principles

Test every concept against these. They come from Threshold's own pillars ([gdd.md](../../../docs/design/gdd.md): metaphorical mechanics, grounded reality, the transition from "Me" to "Us") and from the games the docs name as touchstones: *What Remains of Edith Finch*, *Gone Home*, *Florence*, *Unpacking*.

## 1. The verb carries the feeling

What the player's hands do is the most important line of the story. Tapping in time with someone's heartbeat is attunement. Running perfectly and losing anyway is helplessness. Before you add a story element on top of a mechanic, ask whether the mechanic already says it. If it does, the story element should be quieter.

*Test:* describe the chapter with the sound off and no text. Does the feeling still come through?

## 2. Change the familiar, don't add the new

The House works because the player sees the same room again and it is different. Keep the rooms, the viewpoints and the framing the same across the story. Change what is in them: light, sound, weather, objects, which doors are open. One room seen at five points in the story says more than five rooms seen once (*Unpacking* tells a whole life this way).

*Test:* can the player stand at a viewpoint they know and see the change without being told?

## 3. Absence says more than presence

An empty space where something was, a shut door that used to be open, the sound that stopped. Grief in particular reads through what is missing. Remove before you add.

*Test:* after a sad chapter, is the change mostly something taken away?

## 4. One object, one meaning, one lifeline

Every object the story points at has one job and one life across the chapters: where it arrives, what happens to it, where it ends. Don't let two objects do the same job. Don't introduce a "new" version of something that already exists without a reason that is itself the story. An object set up early should pay off late (the Arrival reprises the Partner and the Tuner for this reason).

*Test:* write the object's lifeline in one line. Does it read like a tiny story?

## 5. Contrast and rhythm

Joy makes the Loss land. Quiet after noise, small after big, warm after cold. Every dark stretch keeps one beat of human awkwardness, and the UI never points at it. Pace the House changes too: a big change after a big chapter, a small one after a small chapter.

*Test:* put the moment next to the one before and after. Are they different enough to feel?

## 6. Navigation is narrative

Where the player can go, and in what order, is part of the story. The first room they can enter, the door they can't open yet, the light down a corridor: these are storytelling, not level design. Lure with light and sound, never with markers. A room that opens should open for a story reason, and a room that shuts should hurt.

*Test:* trace the player's route through the House at each point in the story. Does the route itself say something?

## 7. Grounded first, then poetic

The House is a real house and Leo and Maya are a real couple. Every object and layout choice must make sense in real life (where people actually keep things, what they actually buy and when). The poetry comes from choosing which real thing to show and when, not from unreal things. If a choice needs explaining, it is too clever.

*Test:* would someone who has been through IVF or a miscarriage find it true? Would they find it cheap?

## 8. Never explain, never reward a feeling

The UI doesn't name the metaphor. A chapter doesn't score grief or reward doing a feeling "well". Fail-forward means the story never punishes the player for how they played.

*Test:* is there any text, icon, score or prize that tells the player what to feel? Remove it.

## 9. Restraint costs less and lands harder

A small change in the right place beats a big change everywhere. Every idea has a build cost, especially in M1. Prefer the version a solo developer with AI-made assets can make look right from a fixed viewpoint.

*Test:* what is the cheapest version that still carries the meaning?

## 10. Agency without branches

The player shapes tone, not outcome (Stoic/Vulnerable). Let the player choose where to look and when to tap the chapter object, and let the story be the same for everyone. Make the player's small choices feel personal through wording and pacing, not through different facts.

*Test:* could two players compare notes and find the same story told in two voices?
